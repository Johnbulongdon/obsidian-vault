# Friends beta survey — 2026-05-20

Survey source: UntilFire Research Survey Google Forms analytics export.

Latest readout: 19 responses from the PDF export shared on 2026-05-21. Treat findings as directional because this is still a small friends/family-style sample.

## Executive summary

- The strongest signal is not “I need another FIRE calculator.” It is “tell me what to do with my current situation.”
- Respondents think about financial independence more often than expected: weekly is the largest group, with a meaningful daily group.
- The hardest problems are investing confidence, income growth, and tracking investments — more than pure budgeting.
- Most users still rely on banking apps, but banking apps do not answer “am I okay?” or “what should I do next?”
- Simplicity matters, but users are willing to accept some depth if the product stays clear and practical.
- Willingness to pay exists, but high-price confidence is weak in this sample. The paid offer needs to be earned after the free value moment and framed around ongoing monthly guidance.

## Audience snapshot

19 responses.

Age range:

- 25–34: 73.7%
- 18–24: 21.1%
- Other older group: 5.3%

Current situation:

- Salaried employee is the majority segment at 57.9%.
- Remaining responses include students, freelancers, and business owners.

Approximate annual income:

- $50k–$100k: 31.6%
- $100k–$250k: 26.3%
- $25k–$50k: 15.8%
- Under $25k: 15.8%
- Prefer not to say: 10.5%

Country/region responses are messy/freeform but skew international, including Hong Kong/hk, USA/United States, China, Taiwan, Australia, and EMEA.

## Financial behavior

How often they think about financial independence / retiring early:

- Weekly: 52.6%
- Daily: 21.1%
- Monthly: 15.8%
- Rarely: 10.5%
- Never: 0%

Current financial goals skew toward:

- Early retirement / FIRE: 36.8%
- Increase income: 21.1%
- Reduce financial stress: 15.8%
- Save more, invest better, escape paycheck-to-paycheck, and build side income appear as smaller segments.

How they track finances:

- Banking app: 15/19, by far the dominant behavior.
- Some use spreadsheets, notes apps, dedicated finance apps, or do not track consistently.
- Takeaway: UntilFire should not try to win first as a generic tracker. It should translate scattered financial data into a clear decision and next move.

## Frustrations in users’ words

Themes from open responses:

- No single/uniform app to manage money centrally.
- Managing accounts across countries is painful.
- Currency volatility matters, especially USD/CNY.
- Travel, food, socialising, transport, and housing make spending feel hard to control.
- Banking categories are not trusted enough; users cannot clearly see where money goes.
- Users underestimate recurring bills/autopays and want boundary reminders.
- Investment uncertainty is a recurring pain.
- Bank fees and avoidable charges are annoying.
- Users want help tracking investments and knowing where/how to invest.

Representative non-PII quotes:

- “I don't know what's going on with my spending, what plans should I have.”
- “Just tell me what to do optimally with my current situation.”
- “Very very simple UX with clean UI and dumbed down features.”
- “I always underestimate how much the bills add up.”
- “If it could amalgamate investments from different sources.”
- “Warning me about the fees ahead of time.”

## FIRE / planning pain points

Which feel hardest:

- Understanding investing: 11/19, 57.9%
- Increasing income: 11/19, 57.9%
- Tracking investments: 8/19, 42.1%
- Staying disciplined: 7/19, 36.8%
- Budgeting consistently: 6/19, 31.6%
- Knowing how much is enough: 4/19, 21.1%
- Fear of market crashes: 4/19, 21.1%
- Comparing progress to others: 4/19, 21.1%

Interpretation: the product should connect the freedom date to investment confidence and income/savings actions, not just expense categorization.

## Previous FIRE tool usage

Have users tried a FIRE calculator or planning tool?

- No: 84.2%
- Tried once or twice: 10.5%
- Yes regularly: 5.3%

This is important: early respondents are mostly not existing FIRE-tool power users. They need beginner-safe explanations and less FIRE jargon.

## What would make a financial planning app useful

Open responses point to:

- Clear “what should I do next?” guidance.
- Tax help.
- Bookkeeping / budget-left-this-week or month visibility.
- Investment/tax advice.
- Tailored investing advice.
- TikTok-like recommendations: proactive guidance instead of users hunting for content.
- Fee warnings.
- Savings management.
- Aggregated investments from different sources.
- Auto-linking bills paid.
- Clean, simple UX with dumbed-down features.
- Integrated financial advice.

## Most valuable product direction

Which would be most valuable:

- AI financial coach: 26.3%
- Spending analysis: 26.3%
- Personalized FIRE roadmap: 21.1%
- Investment projections: 15.8%
- Net worth tracking: 10.5%

No strong signal for community/challenges, side-income planning, or habit/accountability as the primary wedge.

Interpretation: the winning direction is a calm adviser that combines roadmap + spending analysis + investment projections into one monthly next move.

## Simplicity vs depth

Scale result from 18 responses:

- 1: 0%
- 2: 33.3%
- 3: 22.2%
- 4: 33.3%
- 5: 11.1%

Interpretation: do not make the app shallow, but keep the interface very simple. Users want practical depth hidden behind clear defaults.

## Willingness to pay

Would pay for a tool that clearly improved financial planning:

- Yes, under $5/month: 42.1%
- Maybe: 36.8%
- No: 15.8%
- Yes, $5–15/month: 5.3%
- Yes, $15+/month: 0%

Interpretation: this sample is price-sensitive. A $19+/month plan may still work for a better-qualified audience, but this survey does not validate it. For friends/family beta, use pricing research carefully: test value language and intent before pushing a hard paywall.

## High-signal open responses

If one financial problem could magically be solved:

- Save more despite cost-of-living pressure.
- Save a target percentage of annual income.
- Track autopays and reduce unnecessary spending.
- Resolve investment uncertainty.
- Clear credit card debt.
- Avoid bank fees.
- Find the right wealth management plan.
- Increase income.
- Handle major family costs such as kindergarten fees.
- Manage housing costs.

Trusted money advice sources/tools are fragmented and weakly held: some mention content creators, banking apps, SoFi, Rocket Money, YouTube, or no trusted source. There is no dominant trusted financial-planning brand in this small sample.

Follow-up availability:

- Yes: 55.6%
- No: 44.4%

## Product implications

1. Lead with “what to do next” and “monthly moves,” not only a calculator result.
2. Keep the no-login freedom-date result, but make the result screen quickly answer: “Am I on track?” and “What should I do this month?”
3. Beginner FIRE education should be embedded gently in context, not presented as a finance course.
4. The strongest paid value hypothesis is a monthly financial coach/adviser that recommends practical moves across investing, spending, bills/fees, and income.
5. The beta paid offer should be tested softly after value. Do not create early pressure with a hard paywall.
6. A high-price Pro plan needs stronger proof than this sample currently provides. If pursuing $3,000 MRR, validate willingness to pay with a better-qualified segment or a clear paid pilot.
7. Banking-app users already have data views; UntilFire should differentiate by converting data into decisions and freedom-date impact.

## Recommended next product questions

- After seeing your freedom date, would you want investment guidance, income guidance, or spending guidance first?
- What would make you trust an AI financial coach with personal finance decisions?
- Would you prefer a monthly plan, weekly nudges, or a one-time roadmap?
- Which data would you be willing to enter manually before connecting accounts?
- What would feel scary or too invasive?
- Would you pay for a first personalized monthly FIRE plan if it showed 3 specific actions and their freedom-date impact?
